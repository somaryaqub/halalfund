Sharia Compliance Primer

Zakat Utilization in a Community-Based Rent Supplement Program

How zakat funds a portable, client-directed rent supplement that remains faithful to classical fiqh, preserves the dignity and autonomy of the recipient, and stays structurally distinct from the capital of the housing fund.

Prepared by

IslamicFamily

Audience

Shariah reviewers and fund / securities counsel

Status

Program in design, principles for review

Purpose

This primer sets out the jurisprudential and operational logic by which zakat will fund a community-based rent supplement. It is written for two readers who must satisfy themselves independently: the scholar assessing Shariah compliance, and counsel assessing how the zakat pool relates to the fund and its securities.

It answers two questions. First, is the use of zakat for a rent supplement permissible under classical fiqh? Second, how does the supplement stay bound to the eligible person rather than to any property, so that zakat reaches the poor directly and never subsidizes the asset or accrues to investors? The two answers converge on one structural feature, examined at §3.

The framework draws on IslamicFamily's Effective Zakat in Canada report and its Environmental Scan of Rent Supplement Models, both cited at the end. Where this primer states a principle, those documents carry the detail.

§1

The governing principle: zakat is for people, not programs

Zakat is a mandated transfer of wealth to specific classes of eligible recipient named in the Qur'an (9:60). For this program the relevant classes are the poor and the needy, and by extension the indebted and the wayfarer, applied to housing need. A valid disbursement requires tamlik, the transfer of ownership of the funds into the hands of an eligible individual. Zakat is the right of the poor; it does not belong to the organization holding it.

It follows that zakat may not be used to build or capitalize a program, an asset, or a building, even where that program serves eligible people. The entitlement must vest in the person.

“Zakat is for People, not Programs.”

Effective Zakat in Canada

The permissible bridge between the person and the service is agency. An eligible recipient may appoint IslamicFamily as their agent to receive and apply their zakat entitlement toward a defined need. The supplement is therefore always the client's; IslamicFamily administers it at the client's direction. The next section states how.

§2

How zakat reaches the person: agency and settlement of the rent obligation

The program relies on wakalah, agency. The eligible client formally appoints IslamicFamily as their agent (wakil). IslamicFamily then holds the client's zakat entitlement and applies it to the client's rent.

  1. Ownership vests, then the obligation is settled. Because the funds are the client's and are applied to discharge the client's own rental obligation, tamlik is satisfied: the economic benefit and effective ownership pass to the recipient through the settlement of their liability. This is the mechanism relied on by established zakat-housing programs internationally.
  2. Delivered in non-cash form. The supplement is delivered either as a direct payment to the landlord on the client's behalf, or as a tenant-held voucher redeemable only toward rent. Direct-to-landlord is the primary structure, since it gives the clearest assurance that zakat reaches its intended shelter purpose and reduces the risk of diversion or self-dealing. Cash to the tenant is generally avoided.
  3. Chosen for tamlik integrity and accountability. The non-cash form is chosen to preserve the intent of the zakat allocation for shelter, to support fraud prevention and audit, and to keep every dollar traceable from donation to disbursement. It is not chosen as a way to shield a recipient's other benefits; that is a separate question, addressed honestly at §6.
§3

Client-bound, not property-bound

This is the central provision of the program, and where the fiqh answer and the securities answer converge.

The supplement attaches to the eligible client, not to any unit, building, or the fund's asset. The client retains the autonomy to apply the support toward any eligible private or non-profit landlord, not only a property owned or managed by IslamicFamily. Once selected, the supplement is theirs. If the client relocates, the supplement travels with them; it is not forfeited to the property and does not remain with a unit for the next occupant.

Why this is decisive for the scholar

Portability is the clearest available evidence of genuine tamlik. If the benefit could not leave the property, one could reasonably ask whether the zakat had really been transferred to the person or had instead serviced an asset. The client's freedom to leave, and to take the supplement, demonstrates that ownership sits with the recipient. It is the person, not the building, that holds the right.

Why this is decisive for counsel

Note for fund and securities counsel

Because the supplement is portable and bound to the person, it is a demand-side benefit rather than an asset subsidy. Underwriters and CMHC treat a portable supplement as negligible to a property's revenue, precisely because the tenant can leave, so it does not guarantee the property's net operating income, does not derisk the asset, and does not accrue to the fund or its investors. The zakat pool is not a source of return on the securities.

Operational separation. Zakat is held and reported as a restricted fund, segregated from investment capital, carried as a liability until disbursed and then expensed to the specific zakat fund on settlement, consistent with tamlik. Independent oversight runs through a dual-committee structure (an audit committee for financial review and a Shariah supervisory board for jurisprudential review). Final flow-of-funds mechanics and the documentation of client agency are to be settled with counsel and reflected in the offering materials.

Autonomy is a Shariah value, not a convenience

The insistence on choice is part of the obligation, not a policy nicety layered on top of it. The Effective Zakat report is explicit that dignity is an utmost value in zakat and that dignity flows from autonomy.

“Dignity comes from autonomy in choices, and from being treated with courtesy and kindness.”

Effective Zakat in Canada

A candid note on the current market. In practice a client may find few competitive alternatives in the local rental market today. That does not weaken the principle; it strengthens the reason to protect it. The right to leave, and the structure that makes leaving possible, must be real and preserved even where it is seldom exercised. The value of the choice does not depend on how often a client uses it.

§4

Eligibility: a dual framework

Eligibility integrates classical jurisprudence with a recognized Canadian poverty benchmark, assessed at two levels.

  1. Individual, on Shariah grounds. The applicant is assessed against the Nisab (asset threshold), calculated on the silver standard to capture the working poor, together with a net asset test that excludes a primary residence, a necessary vehicle, and basic belongings, and deducts outstanding debts. Zakat is assessed on the individual, reflecting the principle that ownership of wealth is personal.
  2. Household, on need. Once individual eligibility is confirmed, need is measured at the household level against a national poverty benchmark. IslamicFamily's environmental scan operationalizes this with the Statistics Canada Market Basket Measure (MBM), Canada's official poverty line (for reference, the 2024 Edmonton MBM is about $57,564 for a family of four and $28,782 for a single adult). The Effective Zakat primer references the Low Income Measure (LIM); the two should be reconciled to a single stated standard before launch.
  3. Boundaries of responsibility. Zakat is not given to one's own ascendants or descendants, whose support is already a binding obligation, and a household member with wealth above Nisab who is responsible for the applicant's housing is taken into account.
  4. Reassessment. Support is time-limited and reassessed on a defined cycle, typically every 12 to 24 months, with a step-down as the household's stability improves, so that limited zakat is recycled toward others in need.
§5

Eligibility and prioritization are distinct

Eligibility establishes that a person may receive zakat. It does not, on its own, establish that they will, or that they rank ahead of anyone else. Zakat is finite, and in practice more people qualify than can be supported at once, so IslamicFamily keeps the two questions separate: eligibility is a gate, not a place in the queue.

Prioritization among eligible clients is decided by internal criteria developed in consultation with scholars, social workers, and accounting professionals. Consistent with the Effective Zakat approach, these criteria favour clear, criterion-based decisions over open-ended discretion or standing committees, since a defined criterion reduces subjectivity, shortens wait times, and eases the burden on frontline workers and clients alike. Urgent need is met first; beyond that, the criteria are designed to direct limited funds where they do the most to move a household toward stability.

For reviewers, the consequence is that a finding of zakat-eligibility is a statement about entitlement in principle, not a commitment of funds. The prioritization criteria are the mechanism by which finite zakat is stewarded fairly among all who qualify.

§6

Delivery form and a recipient's other benefits: an honest account

IslamicFamily delivers the supplement in non-cash form for the reasons at §2, that is, tamlik integrity, accountability, and directing funds to shelter. It is important to be precise that non-cash delivery is not, in itself, a means of protecting a recipient's other income supports.

Under the Canada Revenue Agency's substance-over-form approach, means-tested assistance provided as an ongoing series is treated as social assistance whether it is paid as cash, as a voucher, or directly to a landlord. The delivery route does not change that classification. Protecting a recipient's other benefits is therefore handled through distinct design levers, set out in the environmental scan: keeping annual assistance within allowable thresholds, using the reporting exemption available for genuine one-time emergency support, coordinated case management and tax-filing support, and advocacy for a provincial exemption comparable to the one already available to designated housing bodies. This distinction matters for both readers, and stating it plainly is part of the program's integrity.

§7

Not a wage, and not conditional

The supplement is the right of an eligible person. It is not payment for work, and not contingent on the client's participation in any program or on their conduct as a tenant beyond the ordinary terms of their own tenancy. Attaching conditions to zakat, or treating it as a wage for activity, is not permissible. Supports that help a client toward self-sufficiency may be offered alongside the supplement, but they are never a precondition of receiving it.

§8

Governance, safeguards, and the development of policy

The program is built on a hybrid of faith-based compliance and Canadian audit controls:

  • Restricted-fund accounting. Zakat is tracked through restricted fund accounting, separate from general revenue and operations, with its own ledger and, where feasible, distinct accounts, so every dollar is traceable from donation to disbursement.
  • Independent oversight. A dual-committee model, an independent audit committee and a Shariah supervisory board, keeps disbursement decisions separate from fundraising and ensures no benefit flows to board members, staff, or their associates.
  • Anti-self-dealing. Portability, proportional allocation across community and external landlords, a cap on the share of zakat directed to this program, and direct-to-vendor payment together prevent internal benefit capture.
  • Administrative cap. Administrative deductions are held to a proportionate limit, commonly cited at up to 12.5 percent of zakat collections, and cover only activities attributable to zakat administration such as intake, verification, case management, and audit.

IslamicFamily is developing the detailed income-assessment policies in collaboration with the Canadian Council of Imams, local scholars, and accounting professionals, so that the standards integrate traditional Islamic wisdom (fiqh), CRA compliance, and best practices in poverty alleviation. The aim is a written, reviewable standard a scholar can certify and counsel can rely on, rather than case-by-case discretion.

§9

Transparency

Consistent with the Nur principle of the Effective Zakat report, IslamicFamily posts its zakat policies publicly, details zakat-related expenditures, and treats zakat as an internally restricted fund when engaging auditors. These practices give donors, clients, scholars, and counsel a common, verifiable record of how zakat is used and how decisions are made.
